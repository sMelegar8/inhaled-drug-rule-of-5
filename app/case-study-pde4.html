Mapping 5,211 PDE4 inhibitors: which chemical families shaped the field?

Forty years of medicinal chemistry on one enzyme, turned into a map. Each compound becomes a fingerprint of its substructures, a k-means model groups similar structures into families, and SQL and statistics show which families are potent, selective and efficient, and when each one was made.

This page is written to be read as a walkthrough of the reasoning, not only of the result: how the question was chosen, what each method actually does, which decision was taken at every fork and what the alternative would have cost, and then how hard the conclusions were pushed before being published.

01

Problem statement

PDE4 is an enzyme that breaks down cAMP, a messenger that keeps inflammatory cells quiet. Blocking PDE4 raises cAMP and dampens inflammation, which is why PDE4 inhibitors are used in COPD (roflumilast), psoriasis (apremilast) and atopic dermatitis (crisaborole), and why inhaled ones such as tanimilast are in development.

The field has a known weakness: nausea and vomiting limit the dose. The PDE4D isoform is widely linked to these side effects, while PDE4B is linked to the anti-inflammatory effect, so chemists have tried for years to make compounds that prefer PDE4B.

ChEMBL holds thousands of PDE4 compounds from hundreds of papers and patents, but as a list of rows it says little. A project team, a new hire or an investor would ask simpler questions: what kinds of molecules exist, which kinds work best, and where is the chemistry going?

Goal

Turn 11,954 raw measurements into a readable map of chemical families, and use SQL and statistics to describe each family's potency, selectivity, efficiency and history.

02

How the question was chosen

The analysis did not start from a hypothesis. It started from a table, and the first job was to find a question that the table can actually answer and that somebody would pay to have answered. This is the part of the work that never appears in a notebook, so it is written out here.

Look at what the data can support, before deciding what to ask

PDE4 in ChEMBL 37: 11,954 measurements on PDE4 targets, from 430 papers and patents, covering 5,211 compounds that have both a structure and a usable potency. The fields available are structure, calculated properties, target isoform, potency, assay type, document and year.

What that rules out immediately: anything needing a clinical endpoint, a dose, a formulation, a company name or a pharmacokinetic measurement. Those columns do not exist here, so a question about them would end in guesswork dressed as analysis.

Write down several candidate questions and filter them

Each candidate was judged on three tests: the data can answer it; a respiratory R&D team would care about the answer; and it takes more than one SELECT, otherwise it is a lookup, not an analysis.

Candidate questionVerdict
Which PDE4 inhibitor is the most potent?rejected One ORDER BY. A lookup, and the answer is dominated by assay noise at the top end.
Does PDE4B selectivity reduce nausea?rejected No side-effect or clinical column exists in this data. Not answerable, at any effort.
Which company leads the field?rejected The document records in this slice carry no reliable assignee, so the answer would be inferred from paper titles.
Can potency be predicted from structure?deferred Answerable, but it is a modelling exercise whose ceiling is the assay noise across 430 papers, and it needs scaffold-based splits to avoid leakage. Kept as a next step, not as the story.
What kinds of molecule exist here, which kinds work, and where is the chemistry going?chosen Answerable from structure, potency, isoform and year alone; it is the question a new project team actually asks; and it needs cheminformatics, unsupervised learning, SQL and statistics together.

Fix the unit of analysis before any computation

The same data can be counted three ways, and they give different answers. A row of activities is one measurement; a compound may have twenty. A paper may report one compound or two hundred. Choosing wrongly here inflates every later p-value.

Decision: the unit is one compound, because the question is about kinds of molecule. Measurements are collapsed to a median per compound and target first. Where a claim could instead be driven by one prolific paper, it is re-tested with the paper as the unit — which is exactly what happened to the selectivity result.

Decide in advance what would count as success, and as failure

An unsupervised model always returns groups, so "it produced 8 clusters" is not a result. Before running anything, success was defined as: the groups recover chemotypes a medicinal chemist already names, and the known drugs land where a chemist would put them — rolipram and piclamilast together, roflumilast with its own descendants.

The failure condition, written first: if the clusters had scattered the known drugs at random, or if their statistics had been indistinguishable, the honest output would have been "substructure fingerprints plus k-means do not resolve this space", and the page would say that. Deciding this beforehand is what stops a null result being quietly reshaped into a positive one.

The transferable part

The method here is not PDE4-specific: inventory the columns, write candidate questions, kill the ones the data cannot support, fix the unit of analysis, and define success and failure before modelling. The same four steps apply to a sales table or a manufacturing log.

04

Data and SQL extraction

The starting point is the cleaned table activity_potency from this project: one median potency per compound, PDE4 target and measurement type, after removing censored values, flagged records and unusable units (see the cleaning steps). One SQL query turns it into one row per compound:

  • GROUP BY collapses the rows of a compound into one; MAX(p_median) keeps its best potency on any PDE4 target.
  • MAX(CASE WHEN target_name = 'PDE4B' THEN ... END) is a pivot: it moves the PDE4B and PDE4D values from rows into their own columns, which makes the selectivity a simple subtraction.
  • The join to molecules adds the structure (SMILES) and calculated properties; compounds without a structure are dropped.

Result: 5,211 compounds from 430 papers and patents, of which 1,183 are very potent (best pIC50 ≥ 8, i.e. IC50 ≤ 10 nM) and 675 were measured on both PDE4B and PDE4D.

Three judgement calls hidden in that query

A query of this kind looks mechanical, but each line encodes a decision that changes the result. These are the ones worth defending out loud:

LineDecisionWhy, and what it costs
MAX(p_median)The compound's best potency, not its average A compound measured on PDE4A, B, C and D has four values; averaging them would punish a selective compound for being weak on the isoforms it avoids. The cost: "best" is optimistic, and the maximum of several noisy numbers is biased upward. Acceptable because every family is treated the same way.
MAX(CASE WHEN … END)Pivot the isoforms into columns Selectivity is a difference between two values that live in different rows. Pivoting makes it a subtraction instead of a self-join. The cost: silent NULL when an isoform was never measured, which is why only 675 of 5,211 compounds have a selectivity value at all.
WHERE canonical_smiles IS NOT NULLDrop compounds without a structure No structure means no fingerprint, so such a compound cannot be placed on the map. The cost: a silent exclusion, so it is counted and reported rather than left implicit.
The trap in the median

The potency is a median per compound and target taken across papers, so one compound's value can blend a 1999 assay with a 2019 one. That is tolerable for describing families, and not tolerable for the selectivity claim, which is why selectivity is recomputed inside single papers in the robustness section.

05

Concepts: a short compendium

Each idea used in the analysis, explained from the basics, with what it means for this dataset.

Chemistry as data

SMILES

A SMILES string writes a molecule as a line of text: atoms as letters, bonds as symbols, rings as matching digits, branches in brackets. Roflumilast is O=C(Nc1c(Cl)cncc1Cl)c1ccc(OC(F)F)c(OCC2CC2)c1. Software such as RDKit reads it back into a molecular graph of atoms and bonds.

Here: every structure on this page, including the ones drawn when you hover a point, is rebuilt from the SMILES stored in ChEMBL.
Chemistry as data

Molecular fingerprints (Morgan / ECFP4)

A model cannot read a graph directly; it needs numbers. A Morgan fingerprint describes a molecule by its small circular neighbourhoods: for each atom, it records the atom, then the atom plus its neighbours (radius 1), then neighbours of neighbours (radius 2). Each neighbourhood is hashed to a position in a list of 2,048 bits, and the bit is set to 1 if that substructure is present.

molecule → { atom environments up to radius 2 } → hash → [0 1 0 0 1 ... 0] (2,048 bits)

Two molecules that share many substructures share many 1-bits. Different substructures can occasionally land on the same bit (a collision), a small loss of detail accepted for speed.

Worked by hand: methoxyethane, CCOC

Four heavy atoms, numbered as RDKit reads the SMILES: C0–C1–O2–C3. These are the exact bits the generator sets, and each line is one substructure:

RadiusCentre atomSubstructure it recordsBit
0C0a CH₃ carbon, nothing else1057
0C3also a CH₃ carbon → the same bit1057
0C1a CH₂ carbon80
0O2an ether oxygen695
1C0CH₃ bonded to a carbon294
1C3CH₃ bonded to an oxygen → now a different bit841
1C1CH₂ between a carbon and an oxygen1238
1O2oxygen between two carbons1005
2C1the whole molecule seen from C1817

8 bits set out of 2,048. Three things in that table are the whole idea. At radius 0 the two methyl groups are indistinguishable and share a bit; at radius 1 they separate, because one sits next to carbon and the other next to oxygen — that is what the radius buys. And at radius 2 only one bit appears: the other atoms' radius-2 neighbourhoods are the same whole molecule, and an environment already recorded is not recorded twice.

Two limits to say out loud

The fingerprint stores presence, not count. Ethylene glycol, OCCO, has more atoms than methoxyethane but sets only 5 bits, because its two halves are identical and collapse onto the same bits. And a bit set to 1 does not identify its substructure uniquely: with 2,048 slots for millions of possible environments, collisions are certain — the trade accepted in exchange for a fixed-length vector that any model can consume.

Here: 5,211 molecules × 2,048 bits, a 10.7-million-cell matrix that is 97.4% zeros: a molecule sets 53.4 bits on average, roflumilast 50. Only 2 of the 2,048 bits are never used by any compound in the set.
Similarity

Tanimoto similarity

The standard way to compare two fingerprints is to count shared bits relative to all bits set in either:

T(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| 0 = nothing in common, 1 = identical fingerprints

For ECFP4, close analogues from the same series often score above about 0.4, while unrelated drug-like molecules typically score around 0.1-0.2.

Calibrate the number on molecules you know

A similarity value means nothing until you have seen what it looks like for pairs whose relationship you can already judge. Computed on the real PDE4 drugs in this dataset:

PairTanimotoHow a chemist would describe them
Roflumilast / tanimilast0.440Same chemotype, one optimised from the other
Rolipram / piclamilast0.387Same era, same catechol core, different right-hand side
Rolipram / apremilast0.165Different chemotypes, both PDE4 inhibitors
Rolipram / roflumilast0.154Both are catechol diethers, yet they score as strangers
Rolipram / crisaborole0.086Unrelated: a boron heterocycle against a pyrrolidinone

The fourth row is the one to remember. Rolipram and roflumilast share the pharmacophore a chemist names first, and the fingerprint still rates them at 0.154 — because ECFP4 counts all local environments, and the rest of the two molecules (a cyclopentyl ether and a pyrrolidinone against a difluoromethoxy and a dichloropyridine amide) have almost nothing in common. A fingerprint measures overall substructure overlap, not shared pharmacophore. This is precisely why the model puts them in two different families, and why that split is chemically defensible rather than a bug.

Here: used to check the clusters afterwards: molecules in the same cluster are more similar (mean 0.181) than molecules in different clusters (0.127). A modest gap, and it is the honest one — see the caveat under "Choosing the number of clusters".
Dimensionality reduction

PCA, and why reduce dimensions

With 2,048 dimensions most bits are rarely set, distances become noisy and algorithms slow down (the "curse of dimensionality"). Principal component analysis finds new axes, the principal components, ordered by how much of the variation between molecules they capture, and keeps only the first few.

Before PCA each fingerprint is divided by its length (L2 normalisation), so that a large molecule with many bits does not dominate just because of its size; distances then behave more like the similarity of the bit patterns.

Why 50, and not 2 or 500

The variance profile decides this, and it is flat — the sign of a space with no dominant axes:

Components keptVariance capturedReading
15.0%No single "main direction" of PDE4 chemistry exists
1022.7%Too lossy to separate chemotypes
5051.4%Chosen: about half the variation, k-means still fast and stable
10066.1%Better fidelity, but noisier distances and a less stable clustering

Reaching the 80% that a textbook might recommend would take over 200 components here, which would defeat the purpose: the point of the step is to remove the thin, noisy directions that make distances in 2,048 dimensions meaningless. 50 is a deliberate compromise, and the fact that it discards 48.6% of the variation is a genuine limitation, not a detail to hide.

Here: 50 components keep 51.4% of the variation. The rest is spread over many small, mostly noisy directions — the first component alone carries only 5.0%.
Machine learning

k-means clustering

k-means is an unsupervised learning method: it groups data without being told the right answer. Given a number of groups k, it repeats four steps:

  1. Place k centres (here with k-means++, which spreads the starting centres apart).
  2. Assign every molecule to its nearest centre.
  3. Move each centre to the average of the molecules assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the assignments stop changing.

It minimises the inertia, the total squared distance of each point to its centre:

inertia = Σ over molecules ‖ x − centre of its cluster ‖²

Because the result depends on the random start, the algorithm is run several times (here 10 starts) and the best run is kept. k-means assumes roughly round, similar-sized groups and needs k to be chosen in advance, two limits discussed below.

Here: k-means runs on the 50 principal components; each cluster is then described by its most typical molecule, the medoid (the real molecule closest to the centre).
Model selection

Choosing k: elbow, silhouette, stability

  • Elbow: inertia always falls as k grows; the "elbow" where it stops falling quickly suggests a natural k. With chemical data the curve is often smooth, as here.
  • Silhouette: for each molecule, compare its average distance to its own cluster (a) with the average distance to the nearest other cluster (b): s = (b − a) / max(a, b) 1 = well inside its cluster, 0 = on a border, < 0 = probably misplaced
  • Stability: run k-means with 5 different random seeds and compare the results with the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 1 means the same grouping every time, 0 means agreement no better than chance. A useful grouping should not depend on luck.
Here: silhouettes are low (0.07-0.15) for every k: chemical families overlap and share substructures, so there are no sharply separated groups. At k = 8, 12.4% of molecules have a negative silhouette — they sit closer to a neighbouring family than to their own, and calling them members is a convention, not a measurement. k = 8 was chosen because it is among the most stable settings (ARI 0.75) while still separating known chemotypes, and because 8 colours stay readable on a map.
Visualisation

t-SNE maps

t-SNE places each molecule on a 2D map so that molecules that are close in the 50-dimensional space stay close on the map. It focuses on local neighbourhoods: perplexity (here 40) sets roughly how many neighbours each point tries to keep nearby.

What a t-SNE map shows reliably: which molecules are neighbours, and tight islands of analogues. What it does not show reliably: the size of islands and the distances between far-apart islands, which can be distorted.

Here: a k-means family often appears as several islands of one colour. That is expected: k-means groups by the 50 components globally, while t-SNE splits a family into its tight series of close analogues.
Validation

Checking clusters with chemistry

  • Murcko scaffolds: the ring systems and linkers of a molecule with side chains removed. The "generic" form also ignores atom and bond types. The 5,211 compounds have 1,350 generic scaffolds.
  • Normalised mutual information (NMI): how much knowing the cluster tells you about the scaffold (0 = nothing, 1 = everything). Here 0.42: clusters follow scaffolds partly, but group related scaffolds together.
  • Enriched motifs: for each cluster, the share of molecules carrying known substructures (for example the catechol diether of rolipram) compared with the whole set.
  • Expert reading: the cluster names below were given by reading each cluster's motifs, medoid and most frequent scaffolds; the code falls back to automatic labels if a rerun changes the clusters.
Pharmacology

Potency, efficiency and selectivity

  • pIC50: −log10 of the IC50 in mol/L. 9 = 1 nM, 6 = 1 µM; +1 means ten times more potent.
  • Ligand efficiency (LE): potency per heavy (non-hydrogen) atom, about the binding energy each atom contributes: LE = 1.37 × pIC50 / heavy atoms (kcal/mol per heavy atom; about 0.3 or more is often considered good)
  • Lipophilic efficiency (LLE): potency minus logP. Potency gained by adding grease raises logP as well, so LLE stays flat; values of 5 or more are often quoted for good candidates. LLE = pIC50 − logP
  • Isoform selectivity: pIC50(PDE4B) − pIC50(PDE4D). +1 means ten-fold more potent on PDE4B, −1 ten-fold more potent on PDE4D.
Statistics

Comparing families

  • Kruskal-Wallis test: do several groups have the same distribution? It works on ranks, so it does not assume normal data. Its effect size ε² = H / (n − 1) runs from 0 to 1 (about 0.01 small, 0.08 medium, 0.26 large).
  • Mann-Whitney test: the two-group version; its effect size, the rank-biserial r, runs from −1 to 1.
  • p-value vs effect size: with thousands of compounds almost any difference is "significant". The effect size says whether it matters.

Six lines of code hide six choices. Each one below had a plausible alternative, and the last column is the part that matters in a review: what the alternative would have changed.

How to turn a molecule into numbers MACCS keys (166 predefined substructures); physicochemical descriptors (MW, logP, TPSA…); a learned graph-neural-network embedding Morgan / ECFP4, 2,048 bits. It encodes exactly what a chemist points at — local substructure — without any training, and it is the field's default for similarity, so the numbers are comparable with published work. MACCS is too coarse: 166 fixed keys cannot distinguish two catechol diethers. Descriptors describe properties, so two structurally unrelated molecules with the same MW and logP would land together — the opposite of what is wanted. A learned embedding needs labels, and would make every cluster unexplainable.
Radius, and bits or counts Radius 3 (ECFP6); count-based fingerprints instead of binary Radius 2, binary. Radius 2 is the standard compromise between local detail and collisions; binary bits keep Tanimoto meaning what everyone else means by it. Radius 3 captures larger fragments but makes the vector sparser and collisions more frequent. Counts would weight a molecule with three amides above one with a single amide, which is a different question from the one asked.
Cluster the bits, or reduce first Cluster directly on Tanimoto distance (Butina); use all 2,048 bits L2 normalise, then PCA to 50 components. It removes thin noisy directions, makes k-means and t-SNE fast on 5,211 molecules, and gives both the same coordinates. Clustering on Tanimoto directly is more faithful to chemical similarity. The honest cost of the choice made: Euclidean distance on PCA-reduced normalised bits only approximates Tanimoto, so the geometry is an approximation of chemical similarity, not chemical similarity itself.
Which clustering algorithm HDBSCAN (density-based, leaves outliers unassigned); Butina (similarity cutoff); Ward agglomerative k-means. It returns a fixed, readable number of groups that can each be coloured, named and queried — which is what a map needs. HDBSCAN would label a large share of this continuum as noise, honest but unusable as a map. Butina's answer moves with the chosen cutoff. Ward was actually run as a check: it agrees only moderately, which is reported rather than hidden.
How many clusters k = 4 (most stable); k = 12 or 16 (finer) k = 8. Among the most stable settings while still splitting chemotypes that a chemist names separately. k = 4 is more stable but merges the roflumilast and rolipram chemotypes, erasing the central historical story. k = 16 fragments series into near-duplicate groups and makes the map unreadable.

Plotting PC1 against PC2 shows only 7.9% of the variation — a shapeless cloud. Choosing the number of clusters

Honest reading

k-means imposes a grouping even when the data form a continuum. The families are useful summaries, checked against chemistry, not natural categories. Two of them (2 and 3) are loose "catch-all" groups with low internal similarity (mean Tanimoto 0.14 and 0.13), while families 7, 4, 8 and 6 are tight (0.41, 0.32, 0.31, 0.29). A family's internal similarity should be read before its statistics are quoted: a median taken over a loose group describes a convention, while a median over a tight group describes a chemotype.

Read the three panels in the order a reviewer would. The elbow fails first: inertia falls smoothly, so the data offer no preferred k. The silhouette fails second: it rises with k instead of peaking, which is what happens when groups are carved out of a continuum rather than found in it. Only stability says anything useful. It declines as k grows, from 0.80 at k = 4 to 0.49 at k = 16 — a grouping that would mostly reflect the random seed — with two local peaks, k = 4 (0.80) and k = 8 (0.75). With no statistical optimum available, the choice becomes an explicit judgement between those two stable options, decided on chemical grounds and stated as such rather than presented as the output of a criterion.

Results

Q2 · Families differ strongly in potency, and the best are efficient, not just big

  • Potency differs across families (Kruskal-Wallis H = 805, p = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁶⁹, ε² = 0.15, a medium-to-large effect).
  • The most potent families are the aryl-tetrahydrophthalazinones (7, median pIC50 7.8), the roflumilast type (6, 7.7) and the aza-bicyclic carboxamides (5, 7.4). The purine and cAMP-like heterocycles (3) are the weakest (5.6): they resemble the natural substrate and include old, weak xanthines.
  • Efficiency separates them. Family 5 has the best ligand efficiency (median 0.39) and lipophilic efficiency (4.4) with the smallest molecules (median 342 Da). The roflumilast type reaches its potency with large, lipophilic molecules (475 Da, ALogP 5.2) and a lower LLE (2.6).

Q4 · The newest family is the PDE4B-selective one

  • Family 5 has a median PDE4B − PDE4D difference of +1.41 (about 25-fold), against +0.33 for all other families (Mann-Whitney, r = +0.58, p = 1.8 × 10⁻³⁴); 71% of its measured compounds are at least ten-fold PDE4B-selective.
  • It contains PF-06445974, described in the literature as a PDE4B-preferring compound, and the family's growth after 2015 matches the field's push for PDE4B selectivity.
  • A sanity check: zatolmilast, known as a PDE4D-preferring inhibitor, sits at −2.4, at the opposite end.
Caveat, and what was done about it

The 217 selectivity values of family 5 come from 13 papers, so a few large series weigh heavily, and the PDE4B and PDE4D numbers of one compound can come from different papers and different assays. That is the single weakest point of this case study, so the next section re-tests it four stricter ways instead of leaving the caveat as a disclaimer.

Q5 · Where the known drugs and inhaled programmes sit

  • Family 6, the roflumilast type, holds three generations of the same chemotype at once: roflumilast (oral, approved for COPD), orismilast (oral, in clinical development for inflammatory skin disease) and tanimilast (inhaled, in development; stored in ChEMBL under the name "tranimilast", pIC50 10.4, 688 Da).
  • Rolipram, cilomilast and piclamilast are in family 4; apremilast in family 1; crisaborole in family 2; zatolmilast in family 8.
  • The 248 compounds from papers about inhaled PDE4 inhibitors fall mostly in families 2 (115), 6 (57) and 5 (44): inhaled design built on the roflumilast scaffold and on diverse amide series, consistent with the large molecules found in the Rule of 5 study.
11

Robustness: attacking my own results

Two of the results above are strong enough to be acted on, which is exactly why they deserve to be attacked before anybody else does it. The attacks were written as the objections a referee or an interviewer would raise, and then run as code in src/pde4_fact_check.py.

Objection 1 · "With 5,211 compounds, of course the potency difference is significant"

Correct, and that is why the p-value is not the result. H = 805 with p = 10⁻¹⁶⁹ says almost nothing: at this sample size a trivial difference would also clear any threshold. The quantity that carries meaning is ε² = 0.154, which says family membership accounts for about 15% of the rank variation in potency — medium to large, and worth reporting. The second objection is that one prolific paper could be producing it, so the test was repeated after removing the largest single source, 116 compounds.

Objection 2 · "The selectivity is an artefact of mixing assays and of a few big series"

This is the serious one. Three separate weaknesses are bundled in it, so each was addressed on its own:

  • Mixed assays: a compound's PDE4B value may come from one paper and its PDE4D value from another, years apart. Fixed by recomputing selectivity only where both isoforms were measured inside the same document — same lab, same conditions.
  • The wrong unit of analysis: 217 values from 13 papers are not 217 independent observations. Fixed by collapsing each paper to one value and testing with the paper as the unit, which drops the sample from hundreds to 93.
  • One dominant series: fixed by dropping the paper that contributes the most family-5 values (98 of them) and repeating the paper-level test.

What survives, and what has to be said more carefully

Survives. Both headline results hold. The selectivity result gets stronger under the stricter within-paper definition (r +0.58 → +0.65, median +1.33 log units for family 5 against +0.31 for everything else), which is the expected direction if the signal is real and the cross-paper mixing was adding noise. It also survives the hardest version of the test — one value per paper, minus the biggest paper, 92 papers, r = +0.44, p = 0.020 — where the sample is small enough that an artefact would have collapsed.

Has to be said more carefully. The partition is partly a choice of algorithm. Ward, given the same 50 components and the same k, reproduces only a moderate version of it (ARI 0.48); it keeps 432 of the 436 family-5 compounds together but buries them in a looser cluster of 983, which dilutes the median selectivity to +0.84 and the effect to r = +0.24. So the defensible claim is "there is a PDE4B-selective region of this chemical space, and it is recent and compact" — not "there are exactly eight families and family 5 is one of them".

What was not tested

No correction for multiple testing is applied across the robustness variants, because they are not independent tests of different hypotheses but the same hypothesis under different definitions — reporting all of them, including the weakest, is the correction. The alternative clustering check covers Ward only; Butina and HDBSCAN remain in the next steps.

The questions this work invites, and the answers

If this analysis is presented to a scientific audience, these are the questions that come back. Each answer is grounded in a number from this page, because "I would have to check" is a fine answer for something nobody computed and a poor one for something written in the results.

Explain a molecular fingerprint to someone who is not a chemist.

Take a photograph of every small neighbourhood in the molecule: each atom alone, then each atom with its immediate neighbours, then one shell further out. Keep a checklist of 2,048 boxes and tick the box belonging to each neighbourhood you saw. Two molecules built from similar pieces tick similar boxes, so the molecule becomes a vector a computer can compare.

The honest footnote: the checklist is shorter than the number of possible neighbourhoods, so occasionally two different neighbourhoods tick the same box. Accepted, because it buys a fixed-length vector.

Why k-means? Why not a method that determines the number of clusters itself?

Because the deliverable is a readable map. HDBSCAN would have been more honest about the data — it would mark a large part of this continuum as noise and refuse to assign it — but a map with a third of its compounds unlabelled cannot be handed to a project team. k-means forces every compound into a named group, and the price is paid openly: the grouping is a summary imposed on a continuum, and 12.4% of compounds sit closer to a neighbouring family than to their own.

Your silhouette is about 0.1. Isn't that a failed clustering?

It would be, if the claim were that PDE4 chemistry falls into eight natural categories. That claim is not made. A silhouette near 0.1 is the correct measurement of a space where chemotypes share substructures and blend into each other, and it is reported on the page rather than omitted. The clustering is justified instead by what it recovers: known chemotypes, with the expected drugs in the expected groups, and a within-family similarity (0.181) above the between-family one (0.127).

If the silhouette had been 0.6, I would have suspected the clusters were tracking something trivial, like molecular size.

How do you know the clusters mean anything chemically, rather than being arbitrary?

Three independent checks, none of which the model could see. The clusters correlate with Murcko scaffolds computed separately (NMI 0.42 against 1,350 generic scaffolds). Each family is enriched in a named substructure a chemist recognises. And the known drugs land where a medicinal chemist would put them: rolipram with piclamilast and cilomilast, roflumilast with tanimilast and orismilast, zatolmilast on its own at the opposite end of the selectivity axis.

The last one is a prediction the model could have failed. It was defined as the success criterion before the clustering was run.

You keep only 51% of the variance. Isn't that throwing away half the information?

It is discarding half the variance, and the discarded half is mostly thin noise: the first component carries 5.0%, and reaching 80% would need over 200 components. In a 2,048-dimensional binary space that is 97.4% zeros, keeping every direction makes distances less meaningful, not more. It remains a genuine limitation, and it is listed as one.

I have been told never to trust a t-SNE plot. Why trust this one?

Trust it for neighbourhoods and distrust it for geometry. Which compounds are each other's neighbours is reliable; the size of an island and the distance between two far-apart islands are not, because t-SNE optimises local structure only. Nothing on this page is measured off the map — the clusters come from the 50 principal components and the statistics come from SQL, so the map is a reading aid, not evidence. That is also why one family appears as several islands of the same colour.

Your p-value is 10⁻¹⁶⁹. Should I be impressed?

No, and I would not present it on its own. With 5,211 compounds, a difference too small to matter would also reach that p-value, so the number mostly reports the sample size. The result is the effect size: ε² = 0.154, meaning family membership accounts for about 15% of the rank variation in potency. It also survives dropping the largest contributing paper (ε² = 0.155), which is the check that actually tests the claim.

How do you know the selectivity result is not just one prolific paper?

Because it was tested that way. Collapsing each paper to a single value leaves 12 family papers against 81 others and still gives r = +0.48 (p = 0.008); removing the single largest contributor, 98 values, leaves r = +0.44 (p = 0.020). And restricting to compounds whose PDE4B and PDE4D numbers come from the same document makes the effect stronger (r = +0.65), which is what should happen if the cross-paper mixing was adding noise rather than creating the signal.

What I would not defend is the precise figure of +1.41 log units. Under a different clustering it becomes +0.84, so I quote the direction and the order of magnitude, not the decimal.

Why is selectivity a subtraction and not a ratio?

Because pIC50 is already a logarithm, so the subtraction is the ratio: pIC50(PDE4B) − pIC50(PDE4D) = log₁₀(IC50 on PDE4D / IC50 on PDE4B). A difference of +1.41 log units means roughly 26-fold more potent on PDE4B. Working in log space also keeps the distribution usable — IC50 values span six orders of magnitude, and their arithmetic mean would be governed entirely by the weakest compounds.

Rolipram and roflumilast are both catechol diethers. Your model separated them — isn't that wrong?

It is chemically defensible, and it exposes what the method measures. Their Tanimoto similarity is only 0.154: they share the catechol diether a chemist names first, and almost nothing else, because ECFP4 counts every local environment and the rest of the two molecules differ completely. The fingerprint measures total substructure overlap, not shared pharmacophore. If the task were to group by pharmacophore, this is the wrong representation and a SMARTS-based or shape-based method would be the right one.

What is the weakest part of this analysis?

That the number of families, and therefore the identity of each family, is a decision rather than a finding. Ward clustering on the same components agrees only moderately (ARI 0.48). Everything downstream — a family's median potency, its efficiency, its selectivity — inherits that choice. The second weakest point is that potency mixes IC50 and Ki values from 430 papers with different assay conditions, which puts a floor on how precise any per-family number can be.

This is public data. What would change on a company's internal compounds?

The method transfers unchanged; three things get better and one gets harder. Better: assay conditions are consistent, so potency stops being a median across heterogeneous papers; the unit-of-analysis problem largely disappears, since compounds come from projects rather than publications; and internal series can be positioned against the public families computed here. Harder: internal sets are far more clustered around a few scaffolds, so k-means would mostly recover the project structure already known — which argues for clustering against a public reference space rather than in isolation.

Conclusion

Unsupervised learning on substructure fingerprints turns 5,211 PDE4 inhibitors into eight readable chemical families, and the families tell the story of the field: from substrate mimics, through the catechol diether era, to compact, efficient, PDE4B-selective carboxamides.

The model finds real chemistry

Families match known chemotypes and contain the expected drugs (rolipram, roflumilast, apremilast), even though no labels were given to the model.

Within-family Tanimoto 0.181 vs 0.127 between; NMI with scaffolds 0.42

Potency is a family trait

Family explains a medium-to-large share of potency differences; the best family combines potency with the highest efficiency.

ε² = 0.15; family 5: LE 0.39, LLE 4.4 at 342 Da

Selectivity arrived with a new scaffold

PDE4B selectivity is concentrated in the youngest family rather than spread across old ones.

+1.41 vs +0.33 log units; 0% → 17% of new compounds

Both results survive being attacked

The potency difference holds without the largest paper; the selectivity holds within single papers, at paper level, and with the biggest paper removed. The eight-family partition itself is the part that is a decision, not a finding.

selectivity r = +0.58 published → +0.65 within-paper → +0.48 per paper → +0.44 without the largest; k-means vs Ward ARI 0.48

What this means for a project team

  • A chemical-space map shows at a glance where a new series sits, which families it resembles and how those families performed.
  • Efficiency metrics (LE, LLE) should be reported next to potency: they separate genuinely better chemistry from bigger molecules.
  • The same pipeline, rerun on a company's own compounds, gives a quick and explainable portfolio review.
15

Limitations

  • The number of families is a decision, not a finding. k-means needs k in advance and assumes compact groups; chemical space is a continuum, so families overlap (silhouette about 0.1, and 12.4% of compounds closer to another family than their own). Ward clustering on the same components agrees only moderately (ARI 0.48), so every per-family number inherits the choice of k = 8.
  • Euclidean distance on PCA-reduced normalised bits approximates Tanimoto similarity; it is not Tanimoto. PCA keeps 51.4% of the fingerprint variation, and t-SNE distorts distances between distant islands.
  • Potency is the best median on any PDE4 target, mixing IC50 and Ki and different assay conditions across 430 papers. This puts a floor on how precise any per-family value can be.
  • Compounds are grouped by papers and patents; large series can dominate a family's statistics. For the selectivity result this was tested directly and it held; for potency, efficiency and the timeline it was not tested paper by paper.
  • Inhaled programmes were identified from paper titles only (7 papers), so a programme described without those words in its title is missed.
  • The historical shares assume a compound's first ChEMBL document approximates when it was made. Patent and publication lags differ by company and era.

Next steps

  1. Compare k-means with Butina clustering on true Tanimoto distance and with HDBSCAN, and report agreement as was done for Ward.
  2. Repeat the potency, efficiency and timeline results at paper level, as the selectivity result already is.
  3. Lay the map out with UMAP as well, to see which neighbourhoods are method-independent.
  4. Find activity cliffs: pairs of very similar compounds (Tanimoto ≥ 0.8) with very different potency.
  5. Train a simple model to predict potency from fingerprints, with scaffold-based splits to avoid leakage.
